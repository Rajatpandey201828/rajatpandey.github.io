Cash Reserve Ratio and Stock-Market Dynamics: Investigating the Influence on India’s NIFTY-50 Index

When the Reserve Bank of India locks up more of banks’ deposits, does the stock market feel it? This study isolates the cash reserve ratio from other policy tools and traces its effect on the NIFTY 50.

The question

The cash reserve ratio (CRR) is the share of a bank’s deposits that it must hold with the Reserve Bank of India. Raising it drains liquidity from the banking system, which should raise borrowing costs and slow the flow of money into equities. Most Indian studies estimate the effect of the CRR together with the repo rate, the reverse repo rate and the statutory liquidity ratio, which makes it hard to see what the CRR does on its own.

This study isolates the CRR and asks three things:

  1. Do changes in the CRR significantly influence movements in the NIFTY 50?
  2. How do the short-run and longer-run effects of a CRR change differ?
  3. Does the CRR Granger-cause the NIFTY 50, so that past CRR changes help predict the index?

Policy background

The study window covers a full easing and tightening cycle. The RBI cut the CRR by 100 basis points at the start of the pandemic, restored it in two steps in 2021, and raised it again in 2022 to absorb surplus liquidity as inflation rose.

Data and method

The study uses monthly closing values of the NIFTY 50 from the National Stock Exchange and the CRR from the Reserve Bank of India, and follows a standard time-series workflow in EViews.

  1. Unit-root testsAugmented Dickey–Fuller tests at the 5% level, with lags chosen by the Schwarz criterion.
  2. Lag selectionAkaike, Schwarz and Hannan–Quinn criteria point to two lags.
  3. VAR estimationA two-lag vector autoregression with the NIFTY 50 and the CRR as endogenous variables.
  4. DiagnosticsLagrange multiplier test for residual serial correlation, and a check for homoscedasticity.
  5. Granger causalityTested in both directions, CRR to NIFTY 50 and NIFTY 50 to CRR.
  6. Impulse responsesResponse of the NIFTY 50 to a one-standard-deviation CRR shock over ten months, Cholesky ordering.
  7. Variance decompositionShare of the NIFTY 50’s forecast-error variance explained by CRR shocks over ten months.
  8. Forecast evaluationRMSE, MAE, MAPE and Theil’s inequality coefficient.

Main findings

  1. The CRR leads the market, not the other way round.Changes in the CRR Granger-cause the NIFTY 50, and the reverse relationship is not found. The market reacts to the RBI’s liquidity decisions rather than anticipating them.
  2. Tightening hurts in the short run.A positive CRR shock lowers the NIFTY 50 for the first one to three months. Prices begin to recover after about four to six months and then stabilise as the market adjusts. The 2022 cycle fits this pattern: after the May 2022 increase to 4.5%, the index fell from about 17,400 in April to near 15,300 in June and was back above 17,000 by October.
  3. The CRR matters more at longer horizons.In the first one to three months the NIFTY 50’s forecast-error variance is explained mostly by its own past. The share explained by CRR shocks grows over the ten-month horizon, so the influence of liquidity policy is delayed but substantial.
  4. The model is well specified.Both series are non-stationary in levels and stationary in first differences (ADF statistics of −7.14 for the NIFTY 50 and −7.50 for the CRR). The LM test finds no residual serial correlation at lags one to three (p = 0.30, 0.53 and 0.80). Forecast errors are moderate, with a MAPE of 10.78% and a Theil coefficient of 0.056.

What it means

  • For the RBI: the stock market is part of how CRR changes are transmitted, and abrupt increases can cause short-term market shocks, so market reactions belong in the calculation when the ratio is adjusted.
  • For investors: CRR announcements act as a leading indicator for the index over the following quarter, while the longer-run trend still depends on earnings and the wider economy.
  • Next steps: adding inflation, interest rates, GDP growth and global financial conditions would separate the CRR’s effect from other drivers of the market.
