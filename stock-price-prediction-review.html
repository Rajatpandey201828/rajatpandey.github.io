Stock Price Prediction: A Comprehensive Critical Review of Traditional and Modern Computational Models

Thirty-seven years of forecasting research, read critically. The review asks which models actually predict stock prices, how far the reported gains can be trusted, and what they are worth once trading costs are counted.

The question

Stock price prediction sits where computational finance meets machine learning, and the methods have advanced quickly. The evidence has not kept up. Each study uses its own data, time period, train–test split and error measure, so results are hard to compare across papers. Earlier reviews mostly catalogue models rather than judge them, and almost none confront the tension between reported predictive power and the Efficient Market Hypothesis.

This review sets out to do three things: compare model families on the evidence as a whole, separate statistical accuracy from economic value, and place the findings in a theory of market efficiency that can explain why predictability comes and goes.

How the review was built

The review is a structured critical narrative survey that follows PRISMA conventions for transparency in identifying and screening studies.

  • Sources searched: Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SSRN and arXiv (q-fin and cs.LG), with Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar used to find highly cited work outside those indexes.
  • Included: studies that predict or classify stock prices or returns, report quantitative results or formally apply a model to financial data, and appear in peer-reviewed journals, reputable conference proceedings or well-cited working papers.
  • Excluded: commodity-only and foreign-exchange-only work, and purely theoretical derivations without empirical tests.
  • Result: about 4,820 records were screened down to 112 primary studies covering the United States, India, China, Europe, Southeast Asia, Latin America and Australia.

Geographic breadth was deliberate. Model rankings are not stable across market microstructures, which single-market reviews cannot show.

What it covers

Each model family is reviewed in three steps: its key contributions, a critical evaluation, and the research gaps that remain.

Beyond the model families, the review covers three cross-cutting themes: graph neural networks that model links between stocks, sentiment and multi-modal data from news and social media, and explainable-AI methods that open up black-box forecasts for regulators and auditors.

Main findings

  1. Deep learning wins most controlled comparisons.Sequence models beat statistical and classical machine-learning baselines in the head-to-head studies reviewed. On S&P 500 daily data for 2015–2020, a Transformer reached an RMSE of 41.87 and 69.1% directional accuracy, against 43.25 and 67.0% for an LSTM. Hybrid ARIMA–LSTM models beat either component on its own.
  2. The reported gains are inflated.Publication bias, data snooping across thousands of tested specifications, and leakage between training and test data all push reported accuracy up. Claims of more than 80% daily directional accuracy deserve caution unless pre-registered or independently replicated.
  3. Statistical accuracy is not trading profit.Few studies subtract transaction costs, which run from about 0.05–0.15% per round trip in deep, liquid markets to 0.20–0.60% in less liquid ones. The best average directional accuracy for support vector machines across 55 markets was about 51.6%, an edge that costs can easily erase.
  4. Reinforcement learning is promising but untested in live markets.In one comparison a PPO agent earned a 62% cumulative return against 33% for DQN and 45% for A2C, while A2C had the best risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe ratio 2.11). Most RL studies ignore market impact, slippage and latency, so backtest results overstate what is achievable.
  5. No single model dominates.The right choice depends on how much data is available, the forecast horizon, market depth, the computing budget and whether forecasts must be explained. The review turns this into a selection framework.
  6. The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis fits the evidence better than the static EMH.Predictability appears, is arbitraged away and re-emerges as conditions change (Lo, 2004; Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980). This explains why machine-learning strategies that forecast intraday prices well until about 2009 stopped working afterwards.

Where the field should go next

The gap analysis in each section leads to eight priorities for future research.

  1. Standard benchmarksCurated datasets with fixed, time-ordered splits and agreed accuracy and cost-adjusted metrics.
  2. Cross-regime testingReport worst-case performance across bull, bear and high-volatility periods, not only averages.
  3. Causal approachesGranger causality, instrumental variables and structural models that explain as well as predict.
  4. Interpretable by designModels whose internal structure maps to economic concepts, without relying on post-hoc explanation.
  5. Realistic portfolio RLMulti-asset agents with transaction costs, liquidity limits, risk limits and regulation.
  6. Alternative dataSatellite images, card transactions, patents, filings and hiring data alongside prices.
  7. Online learningModels that update continuously as markets drift, for example Bayesian neural networks.
  8. Transfer across marketsPre-train on large markets and fine-tune on smaller ones where data are scarce.
